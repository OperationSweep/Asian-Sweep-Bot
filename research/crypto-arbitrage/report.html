Research & design brief · crypto arbitrage

Arbitrage Edge Audit

Prepared for Nuru · research date 26 October 2026 · live data sampled 11:29–12:10 UTC · no trades placed, no funds moved

Labels used throughout: VERIFIED means checked against a live order book, source code or a primary document today. INFERENCE means my reasoning from that evidence. ILLUSTRATION means made-up but plausible numbers that show a mechanism, never a forecast.

1. Feasibility verdict

No viable riskless edge for a small operator at retail fees. Do not commit capital yet.

Pure taker-on-both-legs arbitrage between major exchanges, triangular arbitrage inside one exchange, and atomic DEX arbitrage are all either too thin to beat retail fees or are dominated by a few very fast, vertically integrated firms. Today's live books show this directly. The best BTC/USDT cross between Kraken, Crypto.com and Gate was 4.7 basis points, but only about $103 of size sat at that price, worth $0.013 before fees and −$0.87 after them on a $100 trade. Triangular cycles on Crypto.com ran between −2.8 and +0.2 bps before fees, against 22.5 bps of fees.

A 30-minute WebSocket measurement across 7 venues and 9 pairs agreed. 21.8% of venue-pair-seconds showed a headline cross, but zero survived retail taker fees or even a flat 0.10% per leg. Zero of 34 triangular cycles was ever net-positive. Profit only appears near 0.02% per leg, and is small even then (section 4.3).

What would have to change: fees near 0–2 bps per leg (market-maker or high-VIP tiers, or a maker rebate on one leg), less efficient markets (long-tail tokens, new listings, regional fiat premia), or a speed and order-flow advantage a small operator cannot buy. The one design worth testing further is hedged cross-venue market making: rest a limit order on the less liquid venue and hedge on the liquid one. That earns a spread but carries inventory and adverse-selection risk, so it is not riskless arbitrage, and it has to prove itself in paper trading first.

4. Worked net-profit examples and break-even thresholds

net = sell_proceeds × (1 − fee_sell) − buy_cost × (1 + fee_buy) − gas − borrow − rebalance_cost_per_trade − failed_leg_reserve # buy_cost and sell_proceeds come from walking real depth, # so slippage is already inside them and is not added again.

What this says. One in five seconds shows a "headline" arbitrage somewhere. None of it survives the fees a retail account pays, or even a flat 0.10%. An edge only appears near 0.02% per leg. Even then, the $978 ceiling assumes perfect, instant, failure-free capture with inventory everywhere. It is concentrated in routes through Bitstamp and HTX, whose feeds were the slowest and least consistent in the sample (Bitstamp's median feed lag was 570 ms, against 20–90 ms elsewhere), so part of it is probably feed artefact too. At zero fees the ceiling jumps to $48.6k. Over half of that is a USDC/USDT gap of 1–2 bp between KuCoin and OKX on very deep books, which disappears with any fee at all.

Typical headline gaps (best venue pair, before fees): BTC median 5.1 bp (p99 15.5), ETH 1.5 (4.2), SOL 6.0 (10.5), XRP 1.5 (9.6), DOGE 3.9 (6.5), LINK 1.8 (5.5). The cheapest round trip available here costs 15–20 bp.

Triangles: 34 cycles on 7 venues, about 1,500 samples each, produced zero net-positive moments. The best gross was KuCoin USDT→ETH→BTC at a median +0.6 bp (p99 +4.0), against 30 bp of fees.

A broken feed looked like profit. On the second runner, the same code over the same 30 minutes reported 156 "profitable" episodes at retail fees and positive Bitget triangles up to +22 bp. Every one of them used Bitget as the sell side. On the clean runner, Bitget's triangles were all negative. That runner's local Bitget book had desynced. This is exactly how bot screenshots show profits that do not exist. The analyzer now flags feeds whose mid drifts from the other venues, or whose own book is crossed, and re-runs without them.

4.5 DEX and CEX-DEX

Example C: a two-hop atomic DEX arbitrage on Ethereum ILLUSTRATION using sourced inputs. Gas is about 300k units × 0.5 gwei ≈ 0.00015 ETH ≈ $0.35 at the ETH_USDT price of $2,330. That gas assumption comes from the typical 0.1–0.5 gwei base fee reported for early 2026 by ethskills, not a live reading. Pool fees are 5 bp + 30 bp. Gas is not the problem. Winning the block is: top CEX-DEX searchers keep median margins of only 18–28% of revenue after paying builders. Wintermute, SCP and Kayle took about 90% of extracted value by Q1 2025 (AFT 2025).

On cheap L2s it is a spam war. On Base, cyclic-arbitrage contracts used about 51% of gas in Q1 2025, yet only 6.3% of their DEX-touching transactions actually traded (Solmaz et al., AFT 2025). A June 2026 Flashbots study of Base counts 21.4M successful arbitrages against 985.6M failed attempts (Wu & Öz).

4.6 Why "move the coins after you see the spread" fails

A spread that lasts seconds cannot survive a 10–60 minute deposit (blockchain confirmations plus exchange crediting) or a withdrawal review. Every executable cross-venue design therefore needs inventory already sitting on both venues: quote currency where you buy and the coin where you sell. That splits capital four ways, and transfers are only used to rebalance later, at a known cost. The large historic spreads in Makarov & Schoar (Korea premium averaging over 15%) persisted because capital controls stopped this rebalancing, not because nobody noticed.

5. Recommended strategy and architecture

Recommendation. Keep measuring. If the data supports it, test hedged cross-venue market making on 3–5 mid-cap USDT pairs between one deep venue (hedge, taker) and one or two thinner venues (quote, maker). Run it in paper mode first, with inventory pre-positioned. A pure taker-taker detector stays in place as an opportunistic sub-strategy that only fires when net edge clears the threshold. Funding-rate carry is a separate, slower yield option, to be evaluated on its own terms.

Language and concurrency

Start in Python asyncio: one event loop, one task per venue feed, orjson, and uvloop where available. The bottleneck is network round-trip to the venue (typically tens to hundreds of ms from a home connection). Python spends microseconds per depth walk. More threads do not help: the GIL serialises CPU work, and the work is I/O-bound anyway. Move hot paths to Rust (or adopt NautilusTrader) only if logs show internal decision time above about 5% of total latency.

Placement

Measure round-trip from candidate cloud regions to each venue's API and pick the one that minimises the slower leg. Colocation matters more than code speed for taker-taker strategies. INFERENCE Several large venues are hosted in AWS Tokyo; confirm by measurement, not folklore.

Order handling

Use IOC limit orders priced at the depth-walk worst price plus a small tolerance, never plain market orders. Send both legs in parallel. Reconcile from the private fills stream, not from REST polling.

Keys and custody

Trade-only keys, withdrawals disabled, IP whitelist, one sub-account per strategy. Transfers for rebalancing are a separate, manually approved process.

Staged plan with pass/fail gates

  1. Collect (2+ weeks, from your own region and the venues you can legally use). Run arb.collector continuously on a small VPS. Pass: at least 99% of samples per venue with fresh books, clock synced via NTP, and both volatile and quiet days covered.
  2. Record opportunities with arb.analyze, using your actual fee tier. Pass: net-positive episodes that survive at least 1 s must add up, as an upper bound, to at least 3× the expected daily rebalancing cost, with at least 5 distinct episodes a day on the chosen routes. Fail: stop here. That would be the evidence that the edge is not there.
  3. Paper-trade (Hummingbot paper connector or a custom simulator). Inject 250–500 ms latency. A taker fill happens only against the book as it exists at decision time plus latency. Assume a 2–5% leg-reject rate, and model maker fills from trade prints, not quote touches. Pass: net PnL after all costs positive on at least 70% of days, max drawdown under 2%, both-leg fill rate at least 80%, one-sided episodes under 2% of trades, and realised edge at least 50% of the detected edge.
  4. Out-of-sample test. Freeze every parameter, then run on 2+ weeks of new data never used in tuning. Pass: same sign, and at least 50% of in-sample net return per unit of capital.
  5. Live pilot, only after your explicit approval. Cap $500–1,000, trade-only keys, one route, 2 weeks, kill switch tested on day 1. Pass: live slippage within 2 bp of paper, no unhandled one-sided exposure, and drawdown under 2%.

Report at every stage: the share of apparent opportunities that disappear at each filter (headline → net of fees → fillable at size → still there 1 s later → actually filled in paper).

7. Main uncertainties and evidence still needed

  • Your jurisdiction. This decides which venues you can legally use, and the shortlist depends on it. The cloud collector runs from the US, where Binance and Bybit are geo-blocked.
  • Your real fee tier. Several fee figures above come from secondary sources, and they disagree.
  • Sample size. A 30-minute sample on one day is a smoke test, not a distribution. Edges cluster in volatile minutes, so two-plus weeks are needed.
  • Quote age semantics. "Time since last book update" overstates staleness for quiet books and understates it when a venue batches updates. Exchange timestamps and local clocks also need NTP discipline.
  • Maker fill realism. Hedged market making can look great in simulation and lose live to adverse selection. Only a carefully modelled paper stage, then a capped pilot, can settle this.
  • Not measured here: DEX pools, long-tail tokens below the top 10 mid-caps, funding-rate history, and withdrawal-time distributions.
  • Venue risk (insolvency, frozen withdrawals) is not in any PnL figure above, and it is the largest tail risk of holding inventory on several exchanges.
